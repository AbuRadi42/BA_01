Research Presentation

Morphological Efficiency
in Multilingual Language Models

Does teaching a model the grammar of its language — before it sees any data — reduce how much it needs to learn?

And if it does — can we predict how much, for any language, from its grammar alone?

Mini experiment — EN / AR / TR complete Full scale — English complete Full scale — Arabic / Turkish deferred

Sameh AbuRadi · BA_01 Capstone Project

The Question

One hypothesis. Two approaches. Three languages.

Hypothesis

A language model given explicit morphological structure at the token level will learn more efficiently — reaching the same perplexity with fewer training tokens — than one using standard BPE subword tokenization. The richer the grammar, the larger the gain.

If the prediction holds, the size of the gain should be a function of measurable grammar features — opening the door to a formula that estimates compute savings from typology alone.

Baseline

Standard SentencePiece BPE tokenizer. The model sees raw subword fragments with no grammatical signal. This is how most LLMs are trained today.

Morph

Each token is a (root, morphological tag) pair. The model sees the grammatical structure of every word before it learns anything about language. Feature bundles are injected as a second embedding stream.

Why three languages?

English (analytic), Arabic (root-and-pattern — roots are typically trilateral, but also quadrilateral or quinqueliteral), Turkish (agglutinative). If the hypothesis holds, the effect should be strongest where morphology is richest — Arabic and Turkish. English is the control.

What Does Tokenisation Actually Look Like?

The same sentence, split two different ways — once by a standard BPE tokenizer, once by the morphology-aware tokenizer.

Each coloured chip is one token. Morph tokens show the root word and its grammatical tag separately — the model gets both pieces of information at once.

Mini Experiment

Before committing to full-scale GPU training, we ran a small pilot locally to validate the setup and get a directional signal across all three languages.

6

1.8M

5M

Architecture

4-layer GPT, 128 hidden dim, 4 heads. Same design as the full experiment, just smaller. Trained on CPU in ~4 hours total for all 6 models.

Data

~80k Wikipedia sentences per language via HuggingFace streaming. SentencePiece BPE (8k vocab) for baseline. Rule-based morphological segmenter for morph — suffix stripping for EN, root+prefix/suffix for AR, agglutinative suffix chains for TR.

Purpose

Not a substitute for the full experiment. A directional check: does the morph signal help at all? If yes, which language benefits most? The answer shapes how we interpret the full-scale EN results.

Mini Experiment — Metrics 4 & 5 of 5

Grammar accuracy & real-world usefulness

Metric 4 was measured. Metric 5 was not — here is why.

Metric 4 — Grammatical Agreement Accuracy

At each position, we ask: does the model's predicted next token carry the same grammatical tag as the ground-truth token? This measures whether the model has internalised the morphological structure it was given.

English and Turkish reach ~44–46% — well above the random baseline of ~4% (1/23 and 1/63 bundles respectively). Arabic is lower at ~9%, but its bundle space is much larger (270 combinations of prefix×suffix), making random chance ~0.4%. The model is still learning structure — just against a harder target.

Metric 5 — Downstream Task Performance

Can the model's internal knowledge be used for real tasks — like deciding if a sentence is positive or negative, or answering a reading comprehension question?

This tests whether the representations the model built are actually useful beyond just predicting the next word. A frozen model with a single linear layer on top is evaluated on sentiment and QA tasks.

Why this was not measured at mini scale

At 1.8M parameters, the model is too small to build meaningful internal representations. A linear probe on top of a 1.8M-param model would produce near-random results on any real task — not because the morph signal failed, but because the model simply lacks the capacity to encode world knowledge. Measuring this at mini scale would generate noise, not signal. It is only meaningful at full scale (125M+ params), where the model has enough capacity to build representations that transfer.

What the Mini Experiment Tells Us

In plain terms — no jargon.

Turkish — the grammar hint made a huge difference

57% better

Turkish words are built by stacking suffixes — one root word can become dozens of different words depending on what you attach to it. When we told the model "this suffix means past tense" or "this one means plural", it learned the language almost twice as well with the same amount of training data. The grammar hint was worth more than extra data.

Arabic — a meaningful improvement

14% better

Arabic builds words from roots — typically trilateral, but also quadrilateral or quinqueliteral — combined with vowel patterns and affixes. The same root produces dozens of related words across different patterns. Telling the model the root and the pattern helped it recognise those relationships faster. The improvement would likely be even larger with a bigger vocabulary — we had to cut 19% of words as "unknown" due to memory limits.

English — almost no difference

1% difference

English has relatively simple word structure — most words do not change much based on grammar. Standard tokenization already handles English well enough that the extra grammar hint adds almost nothing. This is the expected result, and it makes English the weakest test case for this hypothesis.

The pattern is clear

The more complex a language's grammar, the more the model benefits from being told about it upfront. This is exactly what the hypothesis predicted. The full-scale experiment confirms this for English — and the mini experiment gives us a strong directional signal for Arabic and Turkish.

The bigger picture

Two Questions, Not One

The mini experiment was designed to answer one question — and quietly opened a second.

Question 1 — answered

Does grammar-aware tokenization make a measurable difference?

Yes. The effect is real, it is language-specific, and the ordering matches the prediction. The hypothesis is supported.

Question 2 — opened

Can we predict the size of the gain from a language's grammar alone?

If yes, there is a formula — a function of bundle count, regularity, and a few other structural factors — that maps any grammar to its expected efficiency gain.

Predicted ordering vs measured outcome

LanguageBundlesTypePredicted rankMeasured PPL gainMatch?
English23Analyticweakest~1%✓
Arabic270Templaticmiddle14%✓
Turkish63Agglutinativestrongest57%✓

The ranking is exact — but the magnitudes are not proportional to bundle count alone. Turkish has fewer bundles than Arabic, yet a far larger gain. That asymmetry is the clue. Bundle count is one factor; regularity (clean agglutinative stacking vs noisy templatic morphology) appears to be another. A real formula needs at least both.

If such a formula exists, it would let us estimate the training-cost benefit of grammar-aware tokenization for any language — before spending a single GPU-hour.

Architecture

12-layer GPT, 768 hidden dim, 12 heads. Chinchilla-optimal token budget for model size. Identical architecture for baseline and morph — only the tokenization and embedding strategy differ.

English morph tokenizer

Full derivational + inflectional morphology engine. Produced 27M unique root-POS tokens — embedding table alone ~41GB. Capped to top-50k by frequency. Unknown token rate: 10.68%.

Training data

English Wikipedia — 77.6M sentences, 2.5B whitespace tokens. Same raw text for both baseline and morph. No data augmentation.

What was completed

EN baseline and EN morph both trained to full Chinchilla budget (76,293 steps, 2.5B tokens each). Both evaluated on held-out test set. AR and TR deferred — see later slides.

What the mini experiment suggests

At small scale, AR morph beats baseline by 14% and TR morph beats baseline by 57%. If those ratios hold at full scale — which the mini experiment cannot confirm — the effect would be far more pronounced than what we observed for English. The full three-language experiment is estimated at ~€1,800 total compute.

What Actually Happened

The experiment behind the experiment.

Mar 8 — Start

Dry run passed. Full download started. English hit 2.5B tokens at 77.6M sentences. Arabic and Turkish ran out of sources at 9.6M and 3.75M — a 7x and 15x shortfall. OSCAR-2301 turned out to be gated. MADLAD-400 was the fix.

Mar 10, 10:15 UTC+1 — First Reboot

Provider rebooted the instance mid-run. EN morph preprocessing was at 36M/74M sentences. No checkpointing existed at the time — zero output saved. ~$48 lost. Checkpointing was added, 32 parallel workers enabled.

Mar 11, 03:55 UTC+1 — Pool Deadlock

27/33 chunks done, then multiprocessing.Pool.map() hung — workers alive, 99% CPU idle. Memory pressure on large chunks. Fix: maxtasksperchild=1 to recycle workers after each chunk.

Mar 12, 00:50 UTC+1 — Second Reboot

Instance rebooted again, wiping all processed data. 24/33 chunks had been backed up locally. Re-uploaded, resumed from chunk 24. All 33 chunks done by 13:45. EN baseline trained 18:15–23:20.

Mar 13 — Vocabulary Explosion

EN morph training failed: CUDA OOM trying to allocate 38.74 GiB. The morph tokenizer had produced 27,079,783 unique root-POS tokens — embedding table alone ~41GB. Capped to top-50k by frequency. Unknown rate: 10.68%. Training completed in 5.7 hrs.

Mar 14 — Credits Exhausted

AR and TR baseline models trained to completion (76,293 steps each). Evaluation queued but credits ran out before results were confirmed. AR/TR morph training never started.

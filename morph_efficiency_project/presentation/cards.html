Slide 0 — Title
Morphological Efficiency in Multilingual LMs

"This project starts with one question — and ends, I'll argue, with a second one that matters more."

Two questions, not one. First: does telling a model the grammar of a language before training make it learn better? Second — and this is what I really care about: if it does, can we predict how much from the grammar alone? Can we write a formula?

Three languages — English, Arabic, Turkish. Picked deliberately, not randomly: three different morphological types, and three I happen to speak natively, so I can sanity-check the engines.

The pills are honest. Mini experiment finished across all three. Full scale finished for English. Arabic and Turkish full-scale deferred — I'll explain why and what we have instead.

Slide 1 — The Question
One Hypothesis, Two Approaches, Three Languages

"The hypothesis is simple. The implication is what makes it interesting."

Hypothesis in one breath

If you give a model the grammar upfront — root plus grammatical role — instead of statistical fragments, it should learn faster. And the richer the grammar, the bigger the head start.

Why this matters beyond the result

If the prediction holds, the size of the gain should be a function of measurable features of the language. That's the door to a formula. Today we throw GPU hours at every language as if they were the same. They aren't. A formula would let us estimate the savings before spending a euro.

Why these three

English barely inflects — the control. Arabic builds words from a root and a vowel pattern — templatic. Turkish stacks suffixes in a strict order — agglutinative. Three different morphological mechanisms, three different predictions.

If asked "why not just use a multilingual model?": because mixing languages confounds the variable. I need within-language parity — same architecture, same data, same budget — and only the tokenization changes. That's the only way to isolate the morphology effect.
Slide 1b — Tokenisation Examples
What Tokenisation Actually Looks Like

"Same sentence, two ways. The picture does the work."

Left side: BPE fragments. Frequency-based, no grammar, no meaning per chip. Right side: each chip is a root plus a grammatical tag — explicit structure.

Arabic — point this out

Tap any Arabic chip and you get the وزن — the pattern. That's a layer BPE never sees. Same root, different patterns, different meanings. The morph tokenizer hands the model that distinction for free.

Fewer chips, more meaning

Notice the morph row is shorter. Each chip is doing more work. Hold that observation — it becomes the headline number on slide 7.

Slide 1c — Two Pipelines
Two Training Pipelines

"Everything is held constant — except the one thing I'm testing."

Same Wikipedia data. Same architecture. Same optimizer. Same budget. The only difference is what becomes a token. That's deliberate — anything else would confound the result.

The morph track adds two things: a grammar engine that decomposes each word, and a second embedding stream for the grammatical bundle. Both summed at the input layer. After that, the transformer is identical.

If asked about parameter count: the morph model has an extra embedding table, but the transformer body is identical. The architectural delta is small and accounted for.
Slide 1d — The Three Engines
The Three Grammar Engines

"Each language needed its own engine — because each one packs grammar differently."

What a bundle is

One unique combination of grammatical features. For Arabic, that's {form, voice, person, gender, number, tense} — all of those simultaneously, on a single root.

The numbers — and what they hint at

English: 23 bundles. Turkish: 63. Arabic: 270. More bundles means more grammatical compression headroom — more for the engine to encode that BPE has to learn from co-occurrence. Mark these numbers — they come back on slide 4b.

Why this took 40 days

These engines aren't lookups. Arabic uses the Doha Historical Dictionary for root extraction and wazn pattern matching. Turkish follows Oflazer's two-level morphology for slot ordering. English handles suppletion and derivation. The bulk of the project timeline was here, before any training ran.

If asked "isn't 270 just hard-coding linguistics?": yes — explicitly. That's the entire point. We're testing whether handing the model that structure is more efficient than making it rediscover it from scratch. The whole hypothesis depends on encoding it directly.
Slide 2 — Mini Setup
Mini Experiment

"A small pilot before spending real money — same code, smaller knobs."

Six models, 1.8M parameters each, 5M tokens each. Trained on a CPU in a few hours. Same architecture as the full run, just shrunk.

The point isn't publication numbers. It's a directional signal across all three languages — and a sanity check that the entire pipeline runs end-to-end before I rent GPUs.

If asked about statistical significance: single-seed, treat as directional. The ordering across the three languages is what matters at this scale, not the absolute magnitudes.
Slide 3 — Perplexity
How Confused Was the Model?

"Perplexity is essentially a reading-comprehension score. Lower is better."

The numbers

Turkish: 57% better. Arabic: 14% better — and that's a lower bound because we lost 19% of words to the vocabulary cap. English: roughly flat, exactly as predicted.

The framing

The dashed line is 1.0 — anything below it means the grammar-aware model did better. All three sit below the line. Ordering matches the prediction exactly.

If asked "are the PPLs comparable across tokenizers?": within-language ratio under matched eval text — yes. Cross-tokenizer absolute PPL is not directly comparable, which is why I show TPMU and learning curves alongside.
Slide 3b — Learning Speed
Did It Learn Faster?

"If the dashed line drops below the solid one early — the grammar gave it a head start."

Turkish: pronounced separation. The morph curve drops below baseline within the first few hundred steps and stays there. Arabic: visible. English: nearly parallel — consistent with the perplexity result.

This isn't just lower error at the end. It's lower error earlier. Same data, same compute — the grammar hint converted into faster learning.

Slide 3c — Token Compression
How Much Meaning Per Token?

"Shorthand vs longhand — fewer symbols, each one carrying more."

Tokens-per-meaning-unit. Lower is more efficient — the same content fits in fewer tokens, which means the model's context window holds more meaning.

Why this matters at scale

At inference time, every token costs compute. Shorter sequences mean lower latency, lower cost, longer effective context. This isn't only a training-efficiency story.

Slide 3d — Agreement & Downstream
Grammar Accuracy & Real Usefulness

"The model didn't just memorise — it generalised the structure."

English / Turkish — 44–46%

Random chance is 1 out of 23 or 1 out of 63 — about 2–4%. The model is hitting roughly ten times random. It internalised the bundles.

Arabic — 9.3%, and why that's actually strong

Random chance for Arabic is 1 out of 270 — about 0.4%. 9.3% is roughly 25× random. A harder target, hit harder. Don't let the small number fool the audience.

Metric 5 — not measured, on purpose

Downstream tasks need a model big enough to build representations that transfer. At 1.8M parameters that's noise, not signal. It's a Phase 4 measurement, not a mini one.

Slide 4 — Plain-Language Findings
What the Mini Tells Us

"In one sentence: the more grammar a language packs into its words, the more the model benefits from being told about it upfront."

Turkish 57%, Arabic 14%, English 1%. The ordering is monotonic and matches the hypothesis exactly. That ordering is the most important thing on this slide — more important than the absolute numbers.

English being flat is not a failure. It's the control — it confirms the effect is language-specific, not a method artefact. If English had also jumped 50%, I'd be worried something else was going on.

Slide 4b — Two Questions, Not One ★ NEW
Predictions vs Outcome / The Formula Hypothesis

"This is the slide I want you to remember. The mini experiment answered one question — and quietly opened a more interesting one."

Question 1 — settled

Does grammar-aware tokenization make a measurable difference? Yes. The effect is real, language-specific, and the ordering matches the prediction. The hypothesis is supported.

Question 2 — opened

Can we predict the size of the gain from a language's grammar alone? If yes, there's a formula. A function of bundle count, regularity, and a few other structural factors that maps any grammar to its expected efficiency gain.

The clue is the asymmetry — pause here

Turkish has fewer bundles than Arabic — 63 versus 270 — but a much larger gain: 57% vs 14%. So bundle count alone can't be the predictor. Something else matters. Regularity — Turkish stacks suffixes cleanly, Arabic interleaves them with vowel patterns. A real formula needs at least both factors.

Why this is the contribution

If such a formula exists, it would let us estimate the training-cost benefit of grammar-aware tokenization for any language — before spending a single GPU-hour. That's the scientific contribution this project is reaching toward. The mini experiment didn't find the formula. It made the case that one might exist — and gave us the first three data points.

If asked "what would the formula look like?": I don't know yet — and I shouldn't pretend to with three data points. What I can say is the inputs: bundle count, morphological regularity, derivational depth, possibly type-token ratio. The next experiment is designed to give me the data points to fit it.
Slide 5 — Full-Scale Setup
Full-Scale Experiment

"Same hypothesis, scaled up roughly 70× in parameters and 500× in data."

125M parameters, 2.5 billion tokens — Chinchilla-optimal for that model size. GPT-2-small territory. Rented an A100 80GB on Vast.ai.

Where the trouble started

The morph tokenizer for English exploded to 27 million unique tokens. Embedding table alone would have been 41GB — bigger than the GPU. Capped at the top 50k by frequency. One in ten English words became unknown. That cap is the single biggest caveat in the full-scale results.

Slide 6 — English Learning Curves
English — Learning Curves

"Both curves converge at roughly the same rate. That is the expected result for English."

The grammar hint doesn't help English meaningfully at full scale either — same finding as the mini, just with a much bigger budget. Consistent across scales.

The morph curve sits slightly higher — almost certainly the 10.68% unknown rate inflating the loss. Every unknown is a guaranteed wrong prediction.

Slide 7 — Full-Scale Results
English — Full-Scale Results

"Lead with the caveat. Then point at the one number the caveat cannot touch."

The caveat first — own it

Morph PPL is 55.5 vs baseline 39.4. Morph looks worse. That's the unknown-token penalty — 1 in 10 words is a guaranteed wrong answer. Not a fair comparison. State this before anyone else does.

The number that matters — point at it

4.91× tokens-per-meaning-unit. Each grammar-aware token carries nearly five times more grammatical information than a BPE fragment. This number is independent of perplexity, independent of unknown rate, independent of vocabulary cap. It is the cleanest result in the full-scale run.

100% bundle accuracy

The grammar-aware model perfectly predicted every grammatical tag. It generalised the structure across the entire vocabulary — didn't just memorise.

If asked "isn't 100% just because bundles are injected?": partly — it's a round-trip integrity check. The meaningful claim is the 4.91× compression, not the 100%.
Slide 8 — Arabic & Turkish Deferred
Arabic & Turkish — Deferred, Not Abandoned

"Be precise about what was built and what wasn't. The honest version is the strong version."

What's done

Corpora downloaded. Tokenizers trained. Engines implemented. Both baselines trained to the full Chinchilla budget. Checkpoints sitting on disk.

What isn't

AR and TR morph training never started. Evaluation never ran on the baselines. Credits ran out.

The mini gives us the lower bound

If the mini ratios — AR 14%, TR 57% — hold at full scale, the effect dwarfs English. The full three-language experiment is ~€1,800 of compute. The pipeline is ready to run it the day the budget exists.

Slide 9 — What Actually Happened
The Experiment Behind the Experiment

"Two reboots, one deadlock, one OOM, and a vocabulary that exploded. Every failure was diagnosed and fixed. That's also part of the research."

Spot instances are cheaper than reserved compute, and the trade-off is the provider can reclaim them at any time. I knew that going in. What I underestimated was how much that constraint would shape the engineering — checkpointing every step, designing every script to be resumable. Lesson banked.

The vocab explosion is the real lesson

27 million unique tokens wasn't a bug. It was a design flaw — I treated vocabulary size as a result rather than a constraint. The fix is to design the morphological vocabulary with a hard size cap from the start, by clustering bundles instead of enumerating root×POS combinations. That's a Phase 3 design change, not a patch.

If asked "would you do it on Vast again?": not for anything that takes more than a few hours. Spot is fine for preprocessing, not for training runs that need to survive 24+ hours of provider whim.
Slide 9a — What's Next ★ NEW
From 3 Languages to 9 — Hunting the Formula

"Three points define a line. To find a function, I need more points across more of the morphological spectrum."

What's already built

Nine grammar engines — Mandarin (isolating), English (analytic), Spanish and German (fusional), Turkish and Hungarian (agglutinative), Arabic (templatic), plus Swahili and Basque as outliers. 5,079 tests passing, zero failures. All sitting in the repository, ready to run.

Phase 3 — the next experiment

Eighteen mini models. Nine baselines, nine grammar-aware, same architecture, same budget, only the engine changes. Nine data points across the full morphological spectrum — enough to start fitting a curve instead of just observing an ordering.

The goal beyond the data

Find the function: gain = f(bundles, regularity, derivational depth, …). If we can write that down, then for any language on Earth — even ones we don't have data for — we could estimate how much grammar-aware training would save. That is the contribution this project is reaching toward. Three languages told us the effect is real. Nine languages might tell us its shape.

Why this matters now

Frontier models are spending billions on compute. If a language's grammar can shave even a few percent off training cost, that translates to real money and real energy at scale. And it's free — you only have to encode the structure once. The infrastructure exists. The engines exist. The tests pass. The experiment is ready to run.

If asked "why didn't you run Phase 3 already?": the engines were finished after the BA submission deadline. Phase 3 is the post-capstone continuation — same pipeline, more languages, more data points, real curve fitting.

# 5. Case Studies

---

§4 developed the framework abstractly. §6 reports what Phase 1 observed. This section connects the two. For each of the three languages tested, we describe the morphological system at the level of organisation relevant to the framework, walk through how the grammar engine decomposes representative word forms, and report the numerical inputs — |B(L)|, H(L), ρ(L) — that the engine produces from its 20,000-sentence Wikipedia sample. The three sections are arranged in increasing order of typological distance from the implicitly-assumed English baseline of most transformer literature, and in increasing order of the predicted rebate.

## 5.1 English — analytic

English is the typological baseline against which the framework's predictions will feel least surprising. English grammar is carried largely by word order and function words (articles, prepositions, auxiliaries). Inflectional morphology is limited to a small inventory of suffixes — plural *-s*, possessive *'s*, third-person-singular present *-s*, past *-ed*, progressive *-ing*, comparative *-er*, superlative *-est* — plus a closed set of irregular forms (*go/went*, *be/was*, *child/children*) resolved through a lookup table. Derivational morphology is richer but lives in the lexicon (*research → researcher*) and is treated by the engine as a derivation chain annotated on the root rather than as productive inflection.

A representative decomposition: the word *researchers* decomposes into root *researcher* and feature bundle `pos=NOUN | num=PL`. The word *went* decomposes into the suppletive root *go* with bundle `pos=VERB | tense=PAST`; the surface string bears no morphological relationship to its root, and the lookup table is the only mechanism by which the engine recovers the underlying form. The word *runs* is ambiguous on surface alone between a plural noun and a third-person-singular verb; the engine resolves this via part-of-speech inference and records the ambiguity with an `ambig_3sg=YES` tag.

The observed feature-bundle inventory is small. The engine's 20,000-sentence English Wikipedia sample yields |B(L)| = 22 distinct bundles. The corpus-weighted grammatical entropy is H(L) = 1.43 bits per word, with the corresponding upper bound H_max(L) = log₂ 22 ≈ 4.46 bits. The gap between H(L) and H_max(L) reflects the sharp peakedness of English's bundle distribution: the majority of English word forms are uninflected singular nouns or bare verbs, and the entropy of their features is correspondingly low.

Surface ambiguity in English is localised and well-bounded. The *-s* homograph between noun plural and verb 3SG, identified through corpus statistics, is the principal contributor to H(b | signature). The engine's signature-conditional entropy comes to 0.16 bits; ρ(English) = 1 − 0.16 / 1.43 ≈ 0.89. English therefore exposes approximately 90 % of its grammatical information on the surface form — a recoverability close to, but not at, the agglutinative ceiling, limited chiefly by irregular forms and the handful of genuinely ambiguous surface patterns.

The framework's predicted ordering variable for English is ρ · H ≈ 1.28 bits. This is the smallest of the three languages, and it predicts the smallest rebate. §6 confirms this: the observed Δℒ under morphology-aware tokenization relative to BPE is 0.011 nats, the smallest of the three.

## 5.2 Arabic — templatic

Arabic's morphological system is organised around two orthogonal axes: a triconsonantal root carrying semantic content, and a pattern (**وزن**, *wazn*) carrying grammatical content. A single root — three or four consonants in strict order — combines with any of a fixed set of patterns to produce a family of related words, each interleaving the root consonants with pattern-specific vowels and affixes. The root **ك-ت-ب** (*k-t-b*, "writing-related meaning") combines with pattern I to give **كَتَبَ** (*kataba*, "he wrote"); with pattern III to give **كاتَبَ** (*kātaba*, "he corresponded with"); with the active-participle pattern of form I to give **كاتِب** (*kātib*, "writer"). The same consonantal skeleton carries all of these; the grammatical identity sits in the pattern.

The engine represents Arabic word forms through a three-part decomposition: the surface string, a templatic label naming the pattern (the engine's `template` field: `VERB_TRILATERAL_BARE`, `VERB_AUGMENTED_X`, `NOM_DERIVED`, and so on), and the extracted triconsonantal root. A representative decomposition: the word **استخدم** (*istakhdama*, "he used") decomposes into root **خ-د-م** (*kh-d-m*, serving-related meaning), template `VERB_AUGMENTED_X` (Form X, the *istaf'ala* pattern), and feature bundle `pos=VERB | form=X`. The word **المصرية** (*al-miṣriyya*, "the Egyptian (feminine)") decomposes into root **مصر** (*m-ṣ-r*, Egypt), template `NOM_DERIVED`, and bundle `pos=NOM | def=DEF | role=DERIVED`. Arabic prefixes and suffixes — the definite article **الـ** (*al-*), person markers on verbs, gender/number agreement markers on nouns — appear alongside the pattern as tag values rather than as independent morphemes.

The feature-bundle inventory is substantially larger than English's. On the same 20,000-sentence corpus scale, Arabic yields |B(L)| = 697 observed distinct bundles, with H_max = log₂ 697 ≈ 9.45 bits and corpus-weighted H(L) = 5.44 bits. The higher H(L) reflects Arabic's richer grammatical encoding: gender, number, definiteness, case, person, aspect, voice, and templatic form all live on a single word form, and their realised distribution in natural text is less concentrated than English's.

Structural recoverability is where Arabic's typological character asserts itself most clearly. The observed conditional entropy H(b | signature) is 2.23 bits — substantially higher than English's 0.16 or Turkish's 0.77. This follows from the templatic system's ambiguity. The engine's template labels partition the Arabic lexicon into 29 categorical classes on the 20,000-sentence sample, and multiple distinct feature bundles map into the same template — for instance, `VERB_TRILATERAL_BARE` is carried by forms of many tense–voice–agreement combinations, which cannot be distinguished from the template alone without lexical or contextual resolution. ρ(Arabic) = 1 − 2.23 / 5.44 ≈ 0.59. Just under 60 % of Arabic's grammatical information is recoverable from the surface-pattern signature; the remaining 41 % requires lexicon access or context.

The framework's predicted ordering variable for Arabic is ρ · H ≈ 3.20 bits — higher than English, lower than Turkish. §6 records an observed Δℒ of 0.15 nats, ordinally consistent with the prediction.

## 5.3 Turkish — agglutinative

Turkish is the framework's cleanest case. The language's word forms are built by stacking suffixes onto a root in a strict slot order: root → derivational markers → number → possession → case, or root → derivational markers → voice → tense/aspect/mood → person. Vowel harmony adjusts the surface form of each suffix to match the vocalic profile of the root, but the harmony rules are regular and invertible; the engine's feature tags are harmony-normalised, so *-den* and *-dan* both carry the single tag `case=ABL`. Morpheme boundaries are unambiguous, and the slot-order constraint eliminates combinatorial ambiguity.

A representative decomposition: the word *evlerinizden* ("from your (pl.) houses") decomposes into root *ev* ("house") and feature bundle `pos=NOUN | num=PL | poss=2PL | case=ABL` — a four-slot stack (plural marker *-ler*, second-person-plural possessive *-iniz*, ablative case *-den*). The word *yazıldı* ("it was written") decomposes into root *yaz* ("write") and bundle `pos=VERB | voice=PASS | tense=PAST_DEF`. The surface string is the concatenation of the root and the ordered suffix sequence; the engine's parse is, for well-formed text, the unique analysis consistent with the slot-order grammar.

The feature-bundle inventory is the largest of the three languages by a substantial margin. The 20,000-sentence Turkish Wikipedia sample yields |B(L)| = 1,837 distinct bundles — more than two-and-a-half times Arabic's 697 — with H_max = log₂ 1,837 ≈ 10.84 bits and corpus-weighted H(L) = 4.97 bits. The much higher bundle count reflects the combinatorial nature of Turkish agglutination: each slot admits several values, and their Cartesian product produces a large effective space even with moderate per-slot cardinality. The gap between H_max and H(L) reflects the corpus distribution's concentration on a smaller subset of commonly-used combinations.

Structural recoverability is Turkish's distinguishing property. The conditional entropy H(b | signature) on the 20,000-sentence sample is 0.77 bits, yielding ρ(Turkish) = 1 − 0.77 / 4.97 ≈ 0.85. Turkish does not reach ρ = 1: vowel-harmony variant classes, a small number of irregular verbal stems, and some derivational homographies introduce residual surface ambiguity. But ρ for Turkish sits close to the agglutinative ceiling — higher than English's 0.89 only in the sense that the absolute uncertainty is lower, though the normalised fraction is comparable, and substantially higher than Arabic's 0.59.

The framework's predicted ordering variable for Turkish is ρ · H ≈ 4.20 bits — the largest of the three languages by a comfortable margin. §6 records an observed Δℒ of 0.84 nats, approximately 79 times larger than English's and 5.6 times larger than Arabic's. The ordering by ρ · H matches this ordering. The magnitudes, however, do not: Turkish's Δℒ is disproportionately large relative to its ρ · H. That disproportion — the super-linear shape of the rebate in ρ · H — is the empirical anomaly §6 formalises as the Agglutinative Compounding Effect. The framework, as developed in §4, predicted the ordering these three case studies occupy. What it did not predict is that Turkish, by virtue of its agglutinative structure, would appear to compound the per-bit return on grammatical recoverability in the way Phase 1 observes.

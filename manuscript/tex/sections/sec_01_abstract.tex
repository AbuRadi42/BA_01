\begin{abstract}
Transformer scaling laws (\emph{the empirical curves that predict how a language model's performance grows with its parameter count and the amount of text it is trained on}) are fitted mostly on text from analytic-leaning languages such as English and Mandarin. One reason may be that the AI economy is dominated by firms from the Anglophone world and China; another may be that these two languages happen to suit probabilistic byte-pair tokenisation well (\emph{a statistical method that splits text into recurring character fragments without consulting grammar}). Reasoning-capable language models typically appear only at parameter counts well above what a smartphone or a laptop can host, though exceptions such as Google's Gemma~4 E4B (\emph{an effective 4-billion-parameter on-device model released in April 2026}) suggest the boundary is moving.

This paper asks whether a language's grammatical structure can act as a capacity lever. That is, whether morphology-aware tokenisation, by handing the model grammatical feature bundles (\emph{tense, number, case, person, and so on}) as explicit categorical inputs rather than asking it to recover them from raw characters, rebates a measurable portion of a transformer's parameter budget for a given capability.

We develop a framework in which the predicted rebate is proportional to $\rho(L) \cdot H(L)$, where $H(L)$ is the Shannon entropy of the grammatical feature bundle carried by each word form (\emph{a bit-valued measure of how much grammatical information a single word packs}), and $\rho(L)$ is the share of that information that can be read off the written word itself, without lexical lookup. Both quantities can be calculated from a grammar engine (\emph{a rule-based parser that, given a word, returns its root, its grammatical features, and its part of speech}) before any training run takes place.

A six-model Phase~1 experiment at two million parameters and five million tokens, across English, Arabic, and Turkish, confirms the framework's ordinal prediction. Test-loss reductions under morphology-aware tokenisation come out in the same rank order as $\rho(L) \cdot H(L)$, matching what the grammar engine predicts before training. The magnitude prediction, however, does not hold. The per-bit coefficient $\alpha$ implied by the Hoffmann scaling-law derivative varies by roughly forty-fold across the three languages, with implied $\alpha$ itself growing super-linearly in $\rho \cdot H$.

We call this pattern the \textbf{Agglutinative Compounding Effect}, and show that it admits two mutually exclusive readings. It is either a genuine super-linearity in the framework's functional form, or a sign that English-centric scaling laws break down when applied to languages outside the range they were fitted on. A two-tier Arabic extension splits the rebate into a part recoverable from the written surface and a part recoverable only by consulting the lexicon. This uncovers a companion phenomenon we name the \textbf{Templatic Lexical Surplus}, the extra rebate obtained when a templatic language (\emph{a Semitic language such as Arabic or Hebrew, whose words are built by weaving a consonantal root through a vowel pattern, or \textit{wazn}}) is given explicit access to root identity rather than only to the surface form.

If the Compounding Effect is real, on-device reasoning becomes plausible for morphologically rich languages at parameter counts that English itself does not reach. If instead it is an artefact of the scaling laws, then transformer compute estimates need a morphological correction term. The paper sets out the follow-up experiments that would decide between the two.
\end{abstract}

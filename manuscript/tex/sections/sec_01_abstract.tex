\begin{abstract}
Can the grammar a language already writes on its surface be turned into a lever for building smaller language models? This paper tests whether providing a transformer with an explicit grammatical analysis of each word, its part of speech and inflectional features, instead of the frequency-based fragments of byte-pair encoding, rebates part of the parameter budget a model would otherwise spend learning that grammar from raw text. We train matched baseline and grammar-aware transformers for four languages spanning the typological spectrum, Mandarin, English, Turkish, and Arabic, at compute-optimal scale, and compare how efficiently each regime encodes held-out text.

The verdict is a split, and the split is the finding. For the three languages whose grammar is legible from the written surface, Mandarin, English, and Turkish, a well-trained model recovers that grammar from the text itself and the rebate vanishes; the framework's pre-registered ordering, by how much grammar each language encodes, does not hold. It survives only for Arabic, whose templatic root-and-pattern morphology hides structure off the surface: there the grammar-aware model finishes measurably ahead of its baseline, consistently across four random seeds, and a parameter-matched shuffle control confirms the gain comes from real templatic structure rather than added capacity. Grammar-aware tokenisation is thus not a general capacity lever but a targeted one, compressing precisely where a model cannot otherwise reach the grammar. What the result establishes is existence: a language can carry, within its grammar, structure that, supplied to a model explicitly, spares it part of what it would otherwise spend learning that language. Whether that freed budget extends across the Semitic family, compounds with the compression methods already in use, and ultimately buys reasoning within edge-deployable models is the programme this result opens, not a claim it settles.
\end{abstract}

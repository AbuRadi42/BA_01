\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Two observations about the current state of language modelling sit in quiet tension. The first is that capabilities associated with reasoning (multi-step arithmetic, long-context inference, program synthesis) appear, on a range of measurements, at parameter counts above $10^{10}$ in standard English-centric transformers \citep{wei2022}. The second is that the parameter counts feasible on consumer edge hardware (smartphones, laptops with integrated GPUs, embedded devices) cap well below this threshold, even accounting for 4-bit and lower quantization (roughly $5 \times 10^{8}$ to $2 \times 10^{9}$ parameters on contemporary mobile SoCs). The gap between the two figures is the reason reasoning-capable language models are, at time of writing, cloud-bound services rather than on-device capabilities.

Most treatments of this gap implicitly frame it as a scale problem: a matter of model compression, quantization, distillation, or architectural innovation that will eventually close the distance between capability and deployability. What this framing leaves unexamined is that the figures on both sides of the gap are fit to English. Transformer scaling laws \citep{kaplan2020, hoffmann2022} are calibrated on predominantly English corpora. Benchmarks of reasoning emergence similarly inherit English as their default experimental medium. Whether the same parameter thresholds hold for languages whose grammatical organisation differs substantially from English (those whose word forms pack more grammatical information per token, or whose morphological structure is more recoverable from surface form) is not a settled question; for the most part it is not an asked question.

Responses to the edge--reasoning gap divide along three distinct axes the field rarely names in one breath. The first, and most heavily funded, is \emph{brute-force hardware scaling}: larger datacenter GPUs (Nvidia's H- and B-series), more high-bandwidth memory, more interconnect, more electrical power, on the thesis that capability per dollar bends favourably with scale. The second, more speculative, is \emph{substrate redefinition}: companies such as Extropic\footnote{\url{https://extropic.ai}} are building \emph{thermodynamic sampling units} (inherently probabilistic chips) on the argument that modern AI inference is fundamentally a sampling workload whose deterministic emulation on transistor silicon is physically wasteful. The third, which this paper develops, sits one layer up from both: rather than adding hardware or redesigning its physics, align the tokenization layer to the grammatical structure of the language being modelled, and recover compute on the existing GPU substrate that is currently spent on learning grammar from distributional statistics. The three routes are not mutually exclusive, but they carve the cost problem very differently. Nvidia pays more energy to train the same languages faster; Extropic proposes paying much less energy in a different computational paradigm; morphology-aware tokenization pays nothing extra and recovers compute by changing what the model must learn.

This paper develops the possibility that the parameter threshold for a given capability depends, quantifiably, on the language. Specifically: that a transformer trained under tokenization aligned to a language's morphological structure, where \emph{grammatical feature bundles} are presented as explicit categorical inputs rather than inferred from byte-pair fragment co-occurrence, is spared a portion of the parameter budget a byte-pair equivalent must spend on implicit structural reconstruction.

A \emph{grammatical feature bundle}, in the sense used throughout this paper, is the complete set of grammatical category--value pairs a single word form simultaneously realises. The English \emph{researchers} has bundle $\{\text{NOUN}, \text{number}=\text{plural}\}$; the Turkish \emph{evlerinizden} has $\{\text{NOUN}, \text{number}=\text{plural}, \text{possessor}=\text{2-person plural}, \text{case}=\text{ablative}\}$; the Arabic {\arabicfont استخدم} has $\{\text{VERB}, \text{form}=\text{X}\}$. Bundles are the unit of grammatical information the framework quantifies: they are what a morph-aware tokenizer encodes explicitly, what a byte-pair tokenizer forces the model to reconstruct from distribution, and what the quantities $H(L)$ and $\rho(L)$ introduced next are, respectively, the Shannon entropy and the structural recoverability of.

The size of the rebate the framework argues for is determined by two structural quantities of the language: the Shannon entropy $H(L)$ of its grammatical feature bundles per word form, and the coefficient $\rho(L)$ measuring what fraction of that entropy is recoverable from the surface form. Both quantities are computable from a morphological grammar engine alone, before any training run. The framework predicts that the product $\rho(L) \cdot H(L)$ orders the observed parameter rebate across languages, and under a stated linearity assumption, that a single cross-lingual constant $\alpha$ converts the product into a quantitative prediction.

The paper reports a six-model Phase~1 experiment (English, Arabic, and Turkish, each under byte-pair and morphology-aligned tokenization, identical in every other respect) testing both forms of this prediction. The ordinal prediction holds: the observed efficiency gain is ordered by $\rho \cdot H$ across the three languages, English smallest and Turkish largest, in exact agreement with the framework's forward calculation from the grammar engines. The magnitude prediction does not hold: the implied per-bit coefficient $\alpha$ varies by approximately forty-fold across the three languages under any reasonable parameterisation of the Hoffmann scaling law. The pattern of the deviation is not random: implied $\alpha$ grows with $\rho \cdot H$ itself. Taken at face value, Phase~1 suggests that structurally recoverable grammar buys parameter savings at an accelerating rate, a pattern we name the \textbf{Agglutinative Compounding Effect} and whose interpretation the paper leaves open between two scientifically distinct possibilities.

The paper makes four contributions at once. The first is a framework in which a language's morphology maps to a quantitative prediction about transformer parameter requirements, with inputs computable pre-training. The second is a Phase~1 ordinal confirmation of the framework's prediction across three typologically distant languages. The third is a named empirical phenomenon, the \textbf{Agglutinative Compounding Effect}, consistent with two mutually-exclusive readings: one under which morphology is a compounding capacity lever, and one under which the standard scaling laws require a morphological correction term.

The fourth is a companion phenomenon, the \textbf{Templatic Lexical Surplus}, motivated by two convergent observations. The first was mechanical: the Arabic grammar engine enumerated approximately 270 distinct feature bundles, against Turkish's 63 and English's 23, a spread large enough to raise the question of what that count actually \emph{means} for information encoding density: whether Arabic genuinely packs more grammatical information per word, or whether its bundle taxonomy is simply more combinatorial. The second came from outside computational linguistics: \citet{wei2023native}, imaging 94 native Arabic and German speakers at the Max Planck Institute for Human Cognitive and Brain Sciences, found that Arabic speakers show measurably stronger connectivity in semantic language regions (left temporo-parietal) and stronger inter-hemispheric connections via the posterior corpus callosum, attributed by the authors to the processing demands of Arabic's root-and-pattern morphology. Both lines suggested that Arabic's true per-word information load was larger than its surface behaviour reveals, and therefore that the surface-only $\rho(L)$ of our main framework was systematically under-pricing Arabic's rebate. The two-tier extension tests this directly: a second input stream exposing pure root identity, added alongside the existing surface-bundle and feature-bundle streams, recovers an additional rebate of 0.082 nats at rung~1, holding remarkably stable across the three-rung ladder ($+0.077$ and $+0.058$ at rungs~2 and 3) even as the 1st-tier advantage itself inverts. The measured surplus captures root-identity information only; Arabic's \emph{awz\=an} additionally encode semantic function (agent, patient, instrument, \emph{ma\d{s}dar}, \emph{\d{s}ifa mushabbaha}, Form-II causative, Form-X seeking, and similar first-class semantic classes), which the current engine lumps into coarse templatic categories. Isolating the wazn-semantic-function component as its own measurement, via a natural 3rd-tier extension, is scoped as follow-up in \S\ref{sec:conclusion}. The $0.082 / 0.077 / 0.058$ figures therefore understate the true Templatic Lexical Surplus: they count only the rebate attributable to root identity, not the rebate the wazn's semantic function would additionally contribute if exposed. The full surplus is at least these numbers, and in all likelihood larger.

The paper takes no side between the two readings of the Compounding Effect and specifies the follow-up experiments that would decide.

Edge-hostable reasoning (reasoning-capable language models fitting under smartphone-class parameter budgets) is the motivation for asking whether grammar is a capacity lever, and is the context in which the question's practical stakes are highest. It is not claimed as a result of this paper. It is framed as a conditional implication: \emph{if} the Agglutinative Compounding Effect is a genuine property of morphology-aware language modelling rather than an artefact of the Phase~1 regime, edge-hostable reasoning becomes a plausible near-term prospect for morphologically rich languages at parameter counts English does not currently reach. Whether that conditional holds is the empirical question Phase~1 could not settle on three data points and that the research programme of \S\ref{sec:conclusion} is designed to answer.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. \S\ref{sec:related} places the work in five neighbourhoods of prior art: morphological typology, tokenization in NLP, transformer scaling laws, emergence framing, and the Green AI / linguistic equity / edge ML literature on cost. \S\ref{sec:framework} develops the framework formally. \S\ref{sec:cases} walks through English, Arabic, and Turkish morphology with the grammar-engine decompositions that supply the framework's inputs. \S\ref{sec:results} reports Phase~1, the ordinal validation, the magnitude divergence, and the naming of the Agglutinative Compounding Effect. \S\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the consequences of each interpretation and the limitations that bound confidence in the observation. \S\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes and specifies the discriminating research programme.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The Agglutinative Compounding Effect, as \S\ref{sec:results} describes it, rests on three data points. Whether it survives at larger scale is an empirical question the present study cannot answer. What we can discuss now is the shape of its consequences: how each of the two readings introduced in \S\ref{sec:results-interpretations} would change the way the field thinks about machine-learning cost across languages, together with the limitations that bound our confidence in the observation itself.

\subsection{Consequences under Interpretation A}
\label{sec:discussion-A}

Interpretation~A treats the super-linearity in $\rho \cdot H$ as a real effect. On this reading, tokenization that respects a language's morphology is not a fixed-rate efficiency tool but a compounding one: each additional bit of structurally recoverable grammar yields growing returns in the parameter count a transformer needs to reach a given level of capability. The practical implications fall into three areas.

First, \textbf{running models directly on user devices for morphologically rich languages}, what the engineering literature calls edge deployment, becomes a more plausible near-term prospect than a linear reading of equation~\ref{eq:rebate} would suggest. The shift in the capability-versus-scale curve described by equation~\ref{eq:curveshift}, if it compounds with $\rho \cdot H$, brings the on-device hostability condition~\ref{eq:edgecondition} within reach for agglutinative and highly synthetic languages at parameter counts well below the threshold at which reasoning emerges in English. The framework does not show that reasoning-capable Turkish, Finnish, or Quechua models will fit inside the memory of a 2027 smartphone. It supplies the quantitative condition under which they would, and Phase~1's super-linear shape makes that condition easier to meet.

Second, \textbf{low-resource language modelling} undergoes a conceptual inversion. The standard framing treats low-resource languages as a cost problem: training data is scarce, compute spent per language is expensive, and equity arguments turn on redistributing centrally trained models. Under Interpretation~A, some low-resource languages, namely those whose morphology is both information-dense and structurally recoverable, may be \emph{cheaper} to model to a given capability than English, provided tokenization is aligned with their structure. Equity recommendations shift accordingly: from ``subsidise training on English-shaped pipelines'' to ``build grammar-aligned pipelines for each typological family.''

Third, \textbf{Green AI cost accounting} \citep{schwartz2020, strubell2019}, which tracks the energy and carbon footprint of model training, gains a linguistic axis. A compute budget measured in FLOPs per unit of capability translates differently into each language under the framework, and Interpretation~A widens those differences. Reports that score the environmental cost of training in a language-agnostic way understate the gains available from structure-aware tokenization in languages with high $\rho \cdot H$.

\subsection{Consequences under Interpretation B}
\label{sec:discussion-B}

Interpretation~B accepts the linear form of the framework but holds that the scaling-law derivative used to convert observed $\Delta\mathcal{L}$ into implied $\Delta N$ is unreliable in Phase~1's data-starved regime. On this reading, the Agglutinative Compounding Effect dissolves once training reaches the Chinchilla-optimal data-to-parameter ratio. Assumption~A1 becomes defensible, $\alpha$ becomes the same across languages, and the per-bit rebate the framework predicts collapses to a single cross-lingual constant.

This reading is less rhetorically striking, but it carries its own substantial implication. If Interpretation~B is correct, then \textbf{the Hoffmann/Chinchilla scaling laws, fitted on English-centric corpora, systematically distort cost estimates for other languages in proportion to their typological distance from English.} A morphological correction term, effectively an extra coefficient in equation~\ref{eq:A1} that depends on $\rho \cdot H$, would be needed before those laws could be applied to multilingual training-budget planning. Much of the cited scaling literature treats cross-lingual generalisation as a secondary question. Interpretation~B reframes it as a first-order one. Follow-up work that estimates the cost of multilingual foundation models would need to revise its numbers upward or downward, language by language, from the Hoffmann baseline.

\subsection{What both interpretations share}
\label{sec:discussion-shared}

Both interpretations share one structural commitment: \textbf{language is a quantitative input to ML-economics planning}, not a soft typological label mentioned in passing. The framework makes $H(L)$ and $\rho(L)$ computable from a grammar engine that stands independent of any training run. Cost calculations that ignore them inherit a systematic error whose size depends on which interpretation holds. This paper's contribution is not to choose between A and B, but to show that the choice matters and to specify the experiments that would force it.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion-limitations}

Four limitations bound the confidence the framework warrants at present.

\paragraph{Three data points.}
Phase~1 tests the framework's prediction on English, Arabic, and Turkish, languages spanning analytic, templatic, and agglutinative typology. The ordering of the result holds under this thin evidence base, but questions about its functional form (linear, quadratic, threshold-gated?) need more points to settle. \S\ref{sec:conclusion} sketches the programme that would supply them.

\paragraph{Phase~1's Chinchilla-suboptimal regime.}
All six models are trained at roughly eight times fewer tokens per parameter than the Chinchilla-optimal recipe recommends. This shortfall is the direct source of Interpretation~B's plausibility and cannot be resolved within the current experiment.

\paragraph{Author-native language bias.}
The three languages tested are the three the author speaks personally: Arabic natively, English semi-natively, and Turkish fluently. Engine quality, choice of examples, and the granularity of $\rho$ estimation are all likely to reflect this familiarity. The six additional engines in the repository (German, Spanish, Hungarian, Mandarin, Swahili, Basque) are the main mitigation, but until they are exercised, the author-familiarity effect remains a real confound.

\paragraph{$\rho$ as signature proxy.}
The structural recoverability coefficient as computed in \S\ref{sec:framework-rho} conditions on a signature derived from the engine's own template or root-stripped surface. A more principled estimator, for instance the lexicon-free mutual information between surface affix $n$-grams and grammatical bundles, would reduce the dependence of $\rho$ on the engine and is left to follow-up work.

\paragraph{Semantic storage outside $\rho$'s scope.}
The structural recoverability coefficient $\rho(L)$ measures what a surface-only parser can recover, but morphology encodes grammatical information along two axes that the framework currently collapses into one. The first is the \emph{surface-recoverable} axis that $\rho$ captures: how much of $B(L)$ a rule-based algorithm can decode from the surface string alone. The second is a \emph{lexicon-recoverable} axis: how much additional information becomes recoverable once the parser has access to root meanings and a morphological analyser. For concatenative languages such as English and Turkish these axes nearly coincide, since surface affixation and lexical root largely preserve each other's boundaries. For templatic languages like Arabic they pull apart sharply. Arabic's 270-bundle structural space encodes rich semantic and grammatical information per word form, yet the non-concatenative interleaving that allows this density also hides most of it from a surface-only parser. Our framework rewards Arabic for the bits that survive $\rho$'s filter ($\rho(\text{Arabic}) \approx 0.59$); it does not credit the bits Arabic stores behind that filter, nor the semantic neighbourhoods that root-sharing creates across the Arabic lexicon (the \emph{k-t-b} family of writing-related words, the \emph{s-l-m} family of peace- and submission-related words, and so on). A richer decomposition of $\rho \cdot H$ into surface-recoverable and lexicon-recoverable components is a natural extension. It would let the framework distinguish \emph{morphology a transformer exploits} from \emph{morphology a morphological analyser, or a human reader, exploits}, and would give templatic languages back the credit our current $\rho$ quietly refuses them. \S\ref{sec:conclusion} sketches a two-tier experiment that would quantify this second axis empirically.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:discussion-threats}

The principal threat is that the observed Agglutinative Compounding Effect is an artefact of Phase~1's specific training configuration (a 4-layer, 128-dimension, 5M-token run) rather than a property of morphology-aware language modelling in general. The two discriminating experiments described in \S\ref{sec:results-interpretations} would expose this if it were true. Replication at smaller training scales across more languages, or at larger scales on the same languages, would either preserve the super-linearity (supporting Interpretation~A), compress it (supporting Interpretation~B), or eliminate it entirely (falsifying the framework). Until those experiments are run, the named phenomenon is a hypothesis with empirical support, not a confirmed regularity.

A secondary threat concerns the engines themselves. The Arabic engine's decomposition of nominal forms is imperfect: Form~II \emph{masdars} and \emph{nisba} adjectives are occasionally misclassified. The Turkish engine handles some derivational homographies conservatively. Neither gap is large enough to flip the ordering of $\rho \cdot H$ across the three languages, but both add noise to the conditional-entropy estimates. \S\ref{sec:conclusion} includes engine refinement as part of the research programme.
